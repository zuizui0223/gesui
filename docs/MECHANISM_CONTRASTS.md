# Three competing pathways: water subsidy, nutrient pressure, and contaminant stress

## Why sewage is not automatically an ecological disturbance in one direction

A 2020 global review of **147 studies** of effluent-fed streams reports that many locations have impaired water quality and replacement of sensitive taxa with tolerant ones, yet **high-quality treated effluent can also maintain aquatic habitat and ecological connectivity in dry, water-stressed systems**. These are opposing effects, not one universally beneficial or adverse mechanism. [Global review, Freshwater Biology, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1111/fwb.13519); [Arizona aquatic-invertebrate habitat case study](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10750-020-04343-6).

Treat the following as **testable new gesui hypotheses, not as findings in chocho PR #122**.

| Mechanism | Exposure actually needed | Response and prediction (conditional) | Key falsifier / contrast |
|---|---|---|---|
| **W — Water-supply subsidy** | Outfall-fed stream reaches; observed or modeled perennialization, dry-season discharge, stream intermittency, hydrological connectivity | **In seasonally dry reaches** where treated effluent supplies dry-season baseflow, aquatic/riparian occupancy, habitat persistence or connectivity may rise relative to hydrologically matched dry reaches | No additional perennial-flow persistence or occupancy above rainfall/groundwater and urban control; little extra effect in already perennial wet regions |
| **N — Nutrient enrichment and community turnover** | **Independent N/P load or concentration** plus stream flow, dilution and residence time; connected receiving reach | Shift in **plant/algal trait composition**, eutrophic/tolerant assemblages, turnover; diversity and richness need not rise | No exposure–trait relation after matched flow, cropland N, deposition, urban land use, effort controls; effect equally large upstream or disconnected |
| **C — Contaminant/temperature/oxygen stress** | Chemical/temperature/DO monitoring or chemical-specific fate model, treatment level and dilution | Sensitive aquatic taxa may decline, tolerant organisms replace them; can counteract W's habitat benefit | Effect absent in source-aligned monitoring or not separable from conventional pollution and catchment conditions |

The three paths can co-occur. A highly treated dryland outfall may support sustained aquatic habitat **and** alter nutrients; less-treated effluent can impair water quality. Measure **richness, occupancy, assemblage turnover and trait shifts separately**; never collapse them into one presumed positive response.

## Design: compare process, not merely plant occurrences near WWTP

Use **catchment × reach × season/year** as the exposure unit and **taxon × site × season/year** as the biological unit. Link them through a verified source outfall, receiving reach and downstream position. Keep annual/seasonal discharge and quality source flags, not a constant radius-only N dose. For all-plant models, distinguish freshwater macrophytes, riparian vascular plants and upland land plants.

1. Predefine dry vs permanently wet streams using independent hydrological/seasonal information available **before** the response; contrast potential water effects in the dry group.
2. Build outfall-aligned downstream/upstream and connected/unconnected controls for otherwise comparable urban and agricultural settings. Separate coastal/direct-ocean discharge from inland rivers.
3. Test W's **dryness × dry-season effluent-flow contribution** and N's independently measured **load × connected reach** associations with *plant composition/trait metrics*, controlling for spatial detection and baseline land cover.
4. Test a C pathway only where independently sourced DO/temperature/contaminant measurements exist. Do not derive C from WWTP count or N from model WWp.
5. Evaluate predictive increments on withheld watersheds and regions; perform source-quality and seasonality stress tests. Maintain all-plant analyses independent of butterfly host-only outcomes.

## Physically grounded movement versus ecological connectivity

A pollutant can be transported in rivers, but **that is not proof that plants or insects disperse with it**. [HydroFATE v1](https://doi.org/10.5194/gmd-17-2877-2024), integrated with HydroWASTE and the HydroSHEDS river network, is a promising example of a **contaminant fate** framework (studied for pharmaceuticals/antibiotics). **Its published applications should not be treated as validation of a sewage-N flux model or an organism movement corridor**. N-specific uptake, transformation and removal processes require independent parameterization and validation; terrestrial propagule movement would need separate biological data.

HydroRIVERS excludes many small channels. Sewers, drains and urban canals are also not the same thing as a hydrologically verified river reach. Actual outfalls might discharge directly to sea in small-island/coastal settings; for those, treat **receiving coastal ecology** separately from river transport.

**Publication framing (if supported by new data):** *Wastewater trades off dry-season habitat persistence and chemical/nutrient stress across hydrological contexts*. Unlike the source chocho records, **the competing-pathways claim has not yet been tested in gesui**.
